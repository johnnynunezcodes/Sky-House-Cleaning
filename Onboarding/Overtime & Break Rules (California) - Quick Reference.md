# Overtime & Break Rules (California) — Quick Reference

A cheat sheet for scheduling W-2 hourly employees in California. Pulled from [Employee Pay Structure](Employee%20Pay%20Structure.md); this version just adds worked examples so the "whichever hits first" rule is easy to picture.

## The Rules at a Glance

| Trigger | Rate |
|---|---|
| Over 8 hours worked in a single day | 1.5x |
| Over 40 hours worked in a single week | 1.5x |
| Over 12 hours worked in a single day | 2x |
| 7th consecutive day worked in a week, first 8 hours | 1.5x |
| 7th consecutive day worked in a week, over 8 hours | 2x |

Daily and weekly overtime aren't either/or — whichever threshold an employee crosses first is the one that applies to those hours. A single week can trigger both.

## Example 1: Daily Overtime, Even in a Light Week

An employee works one long day, 9 hours on Tuesday, and 6 hours or fewer every other day that week. Total for the week is well under 40.

That 9-hour Tuesday still owes overtime: 8 hours at the regular rate, 1 hour at 1.5x. The daily 8-hour threshold triggers it on its own, regardless of how light the rest of the week is.

## Example 2: Weekly Overtime, Even With No Long Days

An employee works exactly 8 hours a day, Monday through Friday (40 hours total), then picks up a 4-hour Saturday job.

None of those days individually crossed 8 hours, so no daily overtime applies day-to-day. But the Saturday hours push the week's total past 40, so all 4 Saturday hours are owed at 1.5x, this time from the weekly threshold, not the daily one.

## Example 3: "Whichever Hits First" in the Same Day

An employee works 10 hours on a Wednesday, and the week's running total going into that day was already at 34 hours.

Here the weekly threshold is the one that hits first, not the daily one. The week crosses 40 hours partway through the day: 34 + 6 = 40, so the 40-hour mark lands at the end of the 6th hour worked that day. That means overtime starts at hour 7, two hours before the daily 8-hour rule would have kicked in on its own (which wouldn't happen until hour 9).

So the breakdown for that Wednesday is: hours 1–6 at the regular rate, and hours 7–10 (4 hours, not 2) at 1.5x. Once a threshold is crossed, whichever one gets there first, every hour after it is overtime; the two rules don't stack on top of each other for the same hour, but you also don't get to wait for the later-triggering one.

## Example 4: Double Time in One Day

An employee works a 13-hour day (rare, and worth avoiding by design, but useful to see the math).

- Hours 1–8: regular rate
- Hours 9–12: 1.5x
- Hour 13: 2x

## Example 5: Missed Breaks Stack With Overtime

An employee works a 9-hour day and doesn't get their 30-minute meal break or either of their two paid 10-minute rest breaks (a day this long calls for two rest breaks, one per 4-hour block).

They're owed: 8 hours regular pay + 1 hour at 1.5x (the overtime hour) + 1 extra hour of pay for the missed meal break + 1 extra hour of pay for the missed rest break(s), capped at one premium per break *type* per day regardless of how many individual rest breaks were missed. That's the "real liability" piece, it's a penalty on top of wages, not just more wages.

## The Practical Takeaway

Overtime is a cost you can plan for: a job running long simply costs more per hour past the threshold. Missed breaks are a compliance risk: they're avoidable by pacing the day right, and if they happen anyway, they cost more per incident than overtime does. Both are reasons to leave real breathing room in a daily job list rather than packing it wall to wall.
