# Becoming a Contractor — Internal Note (Johnny's Eyes Only)

**Not for the contractor packet.** This is a heads-up from research, not legal advice — I'm not a lawyer, and this is worth a real conversation with a California employment attorney before you lock in a 1099 contractor model.

## The short version

California uses the "ABC test" (from AB5) to decide whether someone can legally be classified as an independent contractor instead of an employee. To classify a worker as a 1099 contractor, the business has to satisfy **all three** of these:

- **A** — The worker is free from the company's control and direction in doing the work.
- **B** — The worker performs work that is **outside the usual course of the hiring company's business.**
- **C** — The worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, or business of the same nature as the work performed.

## Where this gets risky for a cleaning company specifically

Prong B is the sticking point. If Sky House Cleaning is a cleaning company, and the person you're hiring is cleaning, that work isn't "outside the usual course of your business" — it's the core of it. Several sources researching this specifically call out cleaning/janitorial companies as one of the clearest failure cases for Prong B, alongside delivery and staffing. California's Labor Commissioner has brought real enforcement actions against cleaning and janitorial companies over exactly this.

This doesn't mean it's impossible — some cleaners genuinely run their own independent, multi-client cleaning businesses (own clients, own booking, own pricing, work for you occasionally alongside other work) and could plausibly satisfy the test. But a cleaner who works jobs you book, on your schedule, under your standards and checklist, using your pricing — even with a signed "independent contractor agreement" — is the textbook example of a misclassification risk, regardless of what the paperwork says.

## What's actually at stake

If a worker is later found to be misclassified, the exposure typically includes back wages (including overtime), unpaid payroll taxes and penalties, unemployment/disability insurance contributions, and potential workers' comp gaps if they're hurt on the job. This can also apply retroactively across everyone classified the same way, not just one person.

## What I'd suggest

Talk to a CA employment attorney (or at least a payroll/HR compliance service that knows AB5) before finalizing this. Depending on what they say, you may end up structuring this as W-2 employment instead, or leaning harder into making sure any contractor you bring on genuinely runs an independent cleaning business on the side (own LLC/DBA, own other clients, insurance in their own name) rather than working exclusively through Sky House Cleaning bookings. Worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting into it.

The rest of the onboarding packet (business setup steps, pay, standards, supplies, dress code) is still useful either way — those apply whether someone ends up W-2 or a genuinely independent 1099 contractor. This note is just the piece to resolve before you're relying on the 1099 model at scale.
